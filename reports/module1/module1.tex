\subsection{Architecture and Development Process}
I first decided the overall file structure and the responsibilities of the components. The parser reads the scene description and constructs the in-memory \texttt{Scene}, which holds the camera, film settings, geometry, materials and lights. \texttt{mesh.cpp} loads OBJ data. The pixel loops belong to the concrete integrators, \texttt{whitted.cpp} and \texttt{distributed.cpp}; they call \texttt{Camera::GenerateRay} to map a two-dimensional pixel sample to a three-dimensional viewing ray. My understanding of this mapping drew on the mathematics from Lab~1 and the lectures~\cite[Part~I, pp.~1--2]{cgr-notes}.

The ray is tested against the geometry to find the nearest intersection. The integrator uses its position, normal and material to evaluate illumination~\cite{cgr-transcript}. For each light, a shadow ray tests whether another object blocks the segment between the surface and the light. An occluded point receives no direct contribution from that light. For a visible light on the front side, diffuse shading uses the surface-normal/light-direction cosine, and WSRT uses the halfway vector for Blinn--Phong highlights. Mirror materials instead launch recursive reflected rays. Finally, \texttt{Image} stores the pixels and the image output code writes the result. The common \texttt{integrator.cpp} contains the scene-wide intersection and occlusion queries and the integrator factory. I chose to share components between WSRT and DRT to simplify debugging and keep their behaviour consistent.

\textbf{Formula-guided Code Generation:} I provided formulas and implementation requirements to AI rather than writing every implementation by hand. Examples included pixel-centre sampling, the camera basis and field-of-view mapping, the ray--triangle intersection equation
\[
    \mathbf{O}+t\mathbf{D}
    =\mathbf{V}_0+u\mathbf{E}_1+v\mathbf{E}_2,
\]
and barycentric normal interpolation,
\[
    \mathbf{n}=\operatorname{normalize}
    \bigl((1-u-v)\mathbf{n}_0+u\mathbf{n}_1+v\mathbf{n}_2\bigr).
\]
The triangle equation solves for $t$, $u$ and $v$ using cross products and scalar triple products. I checked components using small examples before integrating them. These are examples of the mathematical input I supplied, rather than verbatim quotations of the original prompts. A specific visual feedback and the division of work are recorded in the reflection section below.

\textbf{Design Decisions:} Recursive reflection uses \( \mathbf{d}_r = \mathbf{d} - 2(\mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{n}_g)\mathbf{n}_g \), where \(\mathbf{d}\) is the incident direction and \(\mathbf{n}_g\) is the geometric normal~\cite[Ch.~8, p.~26]{shirley2018}. The maximum depth is configurable (MD=8 for simple, MD=10 for creative), bounding the work for repeated mirror and dielectric encounters. At each bounce the path throughput is multiplied component-wise by the material reflectance; reflectance below one attenuates successive reflections. A path reaching the depth limit contributes zero radiance.

\textbf{Difficulties Encountered:} The implementation originally mixed incident and outward direction conventions: \texttt{Reflect} expects \(\mathbf{w}_o=-\mathbf{d}\). Passing \(\mathbf{d}\) instead produced the wrong reflected direction. Another defect returned throughput at the depth limit, treating a path weight as emitted light. The fixes use the outward convention and return zero at termination. Analytic checks verify preservation of the tangential direction component, reversal of the normal component, and zero termination contribution.

\textbf{Design Decisions:} Snell's law, \( \eta_i \sin\theta_i = \eta_t \sin\theta_t \), determines the transmitted direction~\cite[Ch.~9, pp.~29--30]{shirley2018}, while the dielectric Fresnel equations assign weights $R$ and $1-R$ to reflection and transmission. The entry test \(\mathbf{n}_g\cdot\mathbf{d}<0\) is performed before orienting the normal, so an exiting ray selects glass-to-air indices. Transmission throughput includes \((\eta_i/\eta_t)^2\) for the radiance convention. If \(\sin^2\theta_t\geq1\), no transmitted direction is generated and $R=1$. For air-to-glass incidence, reflectance rises towards grazing angles; at normal incidence with $\eta=1.5$, $R=0.04$.

\textbf{Difficulties Encountered:} Incorrect direction signs and premature normal flipping caused errors in exit-medium selection. The DRT also discarded the weaker Fresnel branch; both nonzero branches are now evaluated. Numerical checks cover air-to-glass incidence at $30^\circ$ ($\sin\theta_t=1/3$), normal-incidence reflectance of $4\%$, and glass-to-air incidence at $60^\circ$, which exceeds the critical angle and gives TIR.

\subsection{Blinn-Phong Shading}

\textbf{Design Decisions:} Ambient is \( \text{baseColor} \times \text{ambientScale} \). Diffuse uses \( \text{baseColor}/\pi \times \max(0, \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{w}_i) \times I/r^2 \). Specular uses the half-vector \( \mathbf{h} = \text{normalize}(\mathbf{w}_o + \mathbf{w}_i) \) and \( k_s\max(0, \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{h})^{\text{shininess}} \), multiplied by $I/r^2$. The half-vector bisects the outward viewing and light directions~\cite[Part~III, Sec.~3, p.~4]{cgr-notes}. Increasing the exponent concentrates the lobe around alignment with the normal.
\textbf{Example Render:}
Region C in Figure~\ref{fig:wsrt_simple} circles two bright highlights on the green sphere (shininess=60); the red sphere (shininess=120) also has two visible highlights. The scene file specifies two point lights. These localized bright spots differ from the structured scene reflection in the mirror sphere. The red and green spheres also differ in position, colour and specular weight, so their appearance is not a controlled comparison of shininess alone.

\textbf{Design Decisions:} Point lights use intensity \( I \) and distance attenuation \( I/r^2 \). Shadow rays test the finite segment towards each light, with maximum distance \( \text{dist} - 2\epsilon \), so an object beyond the light cannot block it. The origin is offset by \(\mathbf{p}+\epsilon\mathbf{n}_{g,\mathrm{oriented}}\), where \(\epsilon=10^{-4}\max(1,\text{dist})\) and the geometric normal is oriented towards the light-facing side. This avoids numerical self-intersection. Mirror and refraction rays instead use a small offset along their outgoing direction.
\textbf{Example Render:}
Region E in Figure~\ref{fig:wsrt_simple} shows a sharply bounded ground shadow to the right of the green sphere. Region D in Figure~\ref{fig:wsrt_creative} marks the tabletop shadow beside the laptop; the rack also casts narrow, angular shadows. Each ideal point light gives binary visibility at a surface point, and the two lights can produce overlapping shadows with different brightness levels. The light positions and intensities are established by the scene files, since the point lights are not drawn as visible panels.

\subsection{Geometry and Transformations}
\textbf{Design Decisions:} Sphere intersections solve a quadratic, planes use local $z=0$, and individual triangles use M\"oller--Trumbore intersection. The nearest positive hit determines visibility. Rays are transformed by the inverse object matrix and normals by the inverse transpose~\cite[Part~IV, Sec.~3, p.~5]{cgr-notes}. Transformed ray directions are \textbf{not} re-normalized: retaining the same parameter in $\mathbf{p}(t)=\mathbf{o}+t\mathbf{d}$ makes hits comparable across differently scaled objects.
\textbf{Example Render:}
Region F in Figure~\ref{fig:wsrt_simple} identifies the two individual orange triangles; A--D contain analytic spheres, and G is a plane rotated into the ground orientation. These give distinct geometry examples within one view. A numerical scaling check is stronger evidence for parameter consistency: a unit sphere scaled by two, viewed from $(0,0,5)$ along $-z$, is hit at $t=3$ and world-space $z=2$. Inverse-transpose checks also verify that a normal remains perpendicular to a transformed tangent under nonuniform scale.

\textbf{Design Decisions:} PCG32 pseudo-random samples select points uniformly over each rectangular emitter. The area density $1/A$ is converted to a solid-angle PDF \(p_\omega=r^2/(A\cos\theta_{\mathrm{light}})\). Each visible sample contributes \(\beta f\cos\theta_{\mathrm{surface}}L_e/p_\omega\), averaged over samples per light and then over camera samples. The current scenes use one sample per light for each camera sample. Partial visibility of an extended emitter produces a penumbra: different sampled points can be visible or occluded at the same surface point~\cite[Part~VI, Sec.~5, pp.~7--8]{cgr-notes}.

\textbf{Difficulties Encountered:} The rectangle sampling frame originally mixed row and column coordinates, and the sampling normal was not transformed into world space. Checks of sampled corners, world-space normals, area and the solid-angle PDF address these defects independently of image appearance.

\subsection{Phong BRDF and Energy Conservation}
% TODO[brdf-evidence]: tests/module1_checks.cpp 的 Independently integrate 区域；
% 保存日志ID=phong.hemisphere，0/45/80度积分分别为
% 0.999765054384 / 0.765690070758 / 0.360274220793，上界1.0001。
\textbf{Design Decisions:} The BRDF is \( k_d/\pi + k_s \frac{e+2}{2\pi} \max(0, \text{Reflect}(\mathbf{w}_i, \mathbf{n}) \cdot \mathbf{w}_o)^e \), where \( k_s = \text{clamp}(\text{specular}, 0, 1) \) and \( k_d = \text{clamp}(\text{texturedDiffuse}, 0, 1) \times (1-k_s) \). The normalization factor controls the specular lobe's integrated contribution as $e$ changes; the $(1-k_s)$ factor separately reserves a per-channel energy budget for diffuse reflection. The integrator multiplies the surface cosine once, outside the BRDF, and the BRDF is zero outside the supported hemisphere.

\subsection{Defocus Blur and Depth of Field}

% 不把现有数值反推为未发生的个人选择过程。
% Evidence: camera.planar_focus.point_distance，两个样本焦面交点距离实测0。
% output/module1-evidence/*_dof_comparison.png 是旧32/24 spp成对对照；
% 当前128/96 spp主图不能与旧针孔图混称为只改变光圈的对照。
\textbf{Design Decisions:} The thin-lens camera samples a uniform aperture disk using \(r=A\sqrt{u}\) and \(\theta=2\pi v\), where $A$ is the aperture radius. For a pinhole direction $\mathbf{d}$, the focal point is \(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{camera}}+t_f\mathbf{d}\), with \(t_f=D/(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{forward})\). Each sampled lens origin is aimed at this common point on the plane at forward distance $D$~\cite[Ch.~11, pp.~37--39]{shirley2018}. Objects away from this plane are projected to a spread of positions as the lens origin varies; $A=0$ reduces the construction to a pinhole.

\textbf{Difficulties Encountered:} Advancing a fixed distance along every pinhole direction originally formed a spherical focus surface. Intersecting the planar focus surface fixes the off-axis behaviour. A numerical check verifies that rays for a noncentral pixel, generated from different lens samples, meet the same point on the $D=10$ focal plane.
\textbf{Example Render:}
Figure~\ref{fig:drt_simple} uses $A=0.18$, $D=9$ and 128 samples per pixel. The foreground red sphere in region E and the rear checker box in region B have softened boundaries, while the main checker mesh retains more recognizable pattern structure. Figure~\ref{fig:drt_creative} uses $A=0.14$, $D=10$ and 96 samples per pixel; region D selects the green chair behind the screen as a background defocus example, relative to the laptop and bottle arrangement. Grain on a flat-coloured surface is not evidence of defocus. Since no same-scene $A=0$ reference is included, these observations are consistent with the lens model but do not isolate blur from sampling and resolution effects quantitatively.

\textbf{Design Decisions:} Both DRT scenes use extended Reinhard tone mapping per RGB channel, with white point \(W=8\): \(c'=c(1+c/W^2)/(1+c)\). The sequence is HDR sample averaging, tone mapping, clamping to $[0,1]$, and finally sRGB encoding when writing the image. Averaging before this nonlinear mapping avoids averaging already-compressed samples. Tone mapping compresses the radiance range; sRGB encoding converts display-linear values to the output encoding and is a separate operation.

\section{PBRT Scene Compatibility}
The parser supports a PBRT subset: Film, Sampler, Integrator, LookAt, Camera, WorldBegin/End, attribute scopes, Material/NamedMaterial, PPM imagemap Texture, point LightSource, diffuse AreaLightSource, spheres, inline triangle meshes and Translate/Scale/Rotate. The project's named, brace-delimited scene format is also retained and supports OBJ asset loading. Asset paths are resolved relative to the scene file. Standard PBRT FOV uses the smaller image axis; the project block format uses the larger axis, a distinction relevant when translating cameras.

\textbf{Error Handling and Checks:} The \texttt{broken.pbrt} fixture verify diagnostics for a missing texture, an unknown primitive and unsupported object syntax. The \texttt{standard.pbrt} fixture checks standard statements, scopes, multiline mesh arrays, named textures, transforms and point/area emitters. All four reported-scene logs record zero parse warnings and errors. These checks establish behaviour for the supported inputs, rather than full PBRT equivalence: includes, general instancing, animation and nested media are unsupported, and no PBRT-v4 differential rendering comparison has been performed. The current implementation also has no diffuse indirect lighting, so the laptop's emissive screen is not evidence of simulated screen illumination on the table.

\textbf{Verification Summary:} The existing \texttt{make check-module1} suite contains 50 checks covering optical directions and weights, transformed intersections and normals, mesh attributes, light sampling, BRDF energy, camera focus, tone mapping and parser diagnostics. These targeted checks complement the annotated images; they do not establish correctness for arbitrary scenes.

\section{Conclusion}
The simple images provide identifiable examples of mirror reflection, glass refraction, local highlights, shadows, UV textures and geometry. The updated creative reconstruction combines these features in a photograph-inspired laptop and source track scene. Its added tray makes recursive reflections especially clear. WSRT produces sharp point-light shadow boundaries, while DRT adds area-light penumbras, mesh textures and thin-lens sampling; Monte Carlo grain remains visible at the reported sample counts.

The main implementation difficulties were consistent incident/outward direction conventions, correct dielectric entry and exit handling, and preservation of intersection parameters and normals under transformations. Numerical tests supply evidence where visual inspection is insufficient, particularly for TIR, energy bounds and tone-mapping formulas. Useful next steps are a same-scene pinhole reference for defocus, uniformly serial profiling and later BVH acceleration for the mesh-heavy creative scenes.
